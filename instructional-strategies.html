Teaching Philosophy

Effective writing instruction balances high expectations with supportive scaffolding. This page provides evidence-based strategies for teaching composition, with particular attention to the diverse needs of ENG 1010 students, including those in the corequisite ENG 0910 workshop. These approaches emphasize active learning, metacognition, and growth mindset—principles that help all students develop as writers and thinkers.

🌱 Cultivating a Growth Mindset

Many students enter ENG 1010 with fixed beliefs about their writing ability: "I'm just not a good writer" or "I've always been bad at English." Research by Carol Dweck and others shows that mindset matters. Students who believe writing ability can be developed through practice and feedback are more likely to persist through challenges, take intellectual risks, and improve over time.

How to Foster Growth Mindset in Your Classroom

✍️ Praise Process, Not Product

Instead of: "This is a great essay! You're such a talented writer."

Try: "The revisions you made to your thesis show real growth. I can see you're thinking more deeply about your argument."

This reinforces that improvement comes from effort and strategy, not innate talent.

🔄 Normalize Revision

Share your own writing process, including failed drafts and major revisions. Show students that all writers struggle and revise—this is part of the process, not a sign of weakness.

Activity: Show two versions of a published article (early draft vs. final) or share before/after examples of your own writing.

📊 Make Progress Visible

Have students keep portfolios and write reflective cover letters. Ask them to compare Essay 1 to Essay 3. What skills have they developed? Where do they see growth?

Reflection helps students recognize their own progress, which builds confidence and motivation.

💬 Use "Yet" Language

"You haven't mastered thesis statements yet" vs. "You're struggling with thesis statements."

The word "yet" implies that mastery is possible and expected with continued effort.

💡 First-Day Activity: On Day 1, ask students to write about their history as writers—positive and negative experiences. Then discuss: Where do beliefs about "good" and "bad" writers come from? Are these fixed categories, or can writers develop? This sets the tone for a growth-oriented semester.
"The students who think they can't write are often the ones who simply haven't been taught strategies for invention, revision, and editing. Once they learn that writing is a process with learnable skills, they start to see themselves differently." — Composition pedagogy research

📖 Teaching Critical Reading

Students can't write effectively about texts they don't understand. Many students in ENG 1010—especially those placed into ENG 0910—need explicit instruction in active reading strategies. Don't assume they know how to annotate, summarize, or identify an author's argument.

Active Reading Strategies to Teach

1. Annotation & Marking Up Texts

Model this in class! Display a reading on the projector and think aloud as you annotate:

  • Underline key claims and thesis statements
  • Circle unfamiliar words or concepts
  • Use question marks (?) for confusing passages
  • Use exclamation points (!) for surprising or significant points
  • Draw arrows to connect related ideas
  • Write margin notes: "Why?", "Example of X", "Contradicts earlier point"

Then: Have students annotate a short passage independently, then compare annotations in pairs. What did they notice? What did they miss?

2. Summary Practice (Before Analysis)

Students need to understand what a text says before they can analyze what it does. Regular summary practice builds comprehension:

  • One-Sentence Summary: Distill the main argument into one sentence
  • Tweet-Length Summary: Summarize in 280 characters (forces concision)
  • Believing/Doubting Game: Summarize the text as if you completely agree, then as if you're skeptical

3. Reading Questions & Discussion Protocols

Give students a framework for approaching texts:

  • What is the author's main argument or purpose?
  • Who is the intended audience? How do you know?
  • What evidence does the author use? (Examples, data, anecdotes, expert testimony?)
  • What assumptions does the author make? What does the reader need to believe for this argument to work?
  • What's your response? Where do you agree, disagree, or want to know more?
🎯 Activity: Think-Pair-Share Protocol

(15 minutes)

  1. Think (3 min): Students answer one reading question independently in writing
  2. Pair (5 min): Students compare answers with a partner, noting agreements and differences
  3. Share (7 min): Pairs report key insights to the whole class

Why it works: Low-stakes, ensures all students engage, builds toward whole-class discussion.

💡 For ENG 0910 Students: Use workshop time to practice reading strategies with shorter, more accessible texts before tackling full-length readings. Provide sentence starters for summary writing: "The author argues that...", "The main point is..."

🎭 Active Learning Techniques

Lecture has its place, but students learn writing by doing writing. Active learning strategies get students thinking, discussing, and practicing—skills that transfer to their own composing processes.

High-Impact Activities for Writing Classrooms

✏️ Quick Writes (5-10 min)

Start or end class with a brief writing prompt:

  • "What's the most confusing part of today's reading?"
  • "Draft three possible thesis statements for Essay 2"
  • "Explain [concept] to someone who's never heard of it"

Purpose: Activates prior knowledge, generates ideas, checks understanding. Can be shared or kept private.

👥 Peer Workshops

Structured peer review is more effective than open-ended "give feedback":

  • Focused tasks: "Circle the thesis. Is it arguable? Why/why not?"
  • Workshopping one element: Thesis statements, evidence use, introductions only
  • Reverse outlines: Readers create outlines of the draft to reveal structure

🔍 Sentence-Level Workshops

Analyze mentor texts at the sentence level:

  • Display 3-4 sentences from a reading. What do you notice about structure, length, word choice?
  • Have students imitate sentence patterns from strong writers
  • Practice combining short, choppy sentences into more sophisticated constructions

💭 Think-Alouds

Model your own thinking process:

  • How you read a difficult passage
  • How you choose evidence for an argument
  • How you revise a weak paragraph

Experts do much of this automatically; novices need to see the process made visible.

📘 Sample Activity: Thesis Statement Workshop (20 min)
  1. Display 5 sample thesis statements (mix of strong and weak, from student work or created by you)
  2. Small groups evaluate each: Is it arguable? Specific? Interesting?
  3. Rank them from strongest to weakest
  4. Whole-class debrief: What makes a thesis effective? What are common weaknesses?
  5. Application: Students draft/revise their own thesis statements

Discussion Strategies That Work

Strategy How It Works Best For
Fishbowl Discussion Inner circle discusses while outer circle observes and takes notes. Then switch. Ensuring all students participate; modeling active listening
Socratic Seminar Student-led discussion with prepared questions. Instructor is facilitator, not leader. Close reading of complex texts; encouraging student ownership
Jigsaw Groups become "experts" on different texts/topics, then teach others. Covering multiple readings efficiently; peer teaching
Four Corners Post statements in corners. Students physically move to agree/disagree/unsure positions, then discuss. Activating debate; visual representation of different perspectives

🎯 Differentiation & Support Strategies

ENG 1010 classrooms include students with varied preparation, languages, learning styles, and needs. Effective differentiation means providing multiple pathways to success while maintaining high expectations for all students.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL) Principles

Multiple Means of Representation

Present information in various formats:

  • Provide readings in multiple formats (PDF, audio, video explanations)
  • Use visual aids (charts, diagrams, concept maps) alongside text
  • Offer examples and non-examples of concepts
  • Pre-teach key vocabulary and concepts

Multiple Means of Engagement

Offer choices to increase student motivation:

  • Choice of essay topics within parameters
  • Option to write blog posts, scripts, or traditional essays (for low-stakes assignments)
  • Flexible deadlines with student-teacher contracts
  • Opportunities to connect assignments to personal interests

Multiple Means of Expression

Allow different ways to demonstrate learning:

  • Oral presentations of research findings before writing
  • Visual arguments (infographics, posters) alongside written text
  • Video reflections on writing process
  • Multiple draft submissions with focused feedback

Supporting Multilingual Writers

Best Practices for Multilingual/ESL Students

  • Focus on higher-order concerns first: Thesis, organization, evidence before grammar
  • Distinguish between errors and stylistic differences: Not all non-standard English is "wrong"
  • Provide sentence models and frames: "While X argues _____, Y contends _____"
  • Allow extra time for reading comprehension
  • Encourage use of language resources: Translation tools, dictionaries, campus tutoring
  • Celebrate linguistic diversity: Students' home languages are assets, not deficits
💡 Grammar Instruction That Works: Teach grammar in context when students need it for their own writing. Mini-lessons on common errors (comma splices, fragments, subject-verb agreement) are more effective than decontextualized drills. Consider a "grammar workshop" rotation during revision week where students attend sessions on their self-identified needs.

⏰ Classroom Time Management

Effective use of class time means balancing instruction, discussion, and active work. Here's a sample class structure that can be adapted for different modes (traditional, online, hybrid).

Sample 75-Minute Class Structure

Opening (10 minutes)
  • Quick write to activate prior knowledge or respond to homework reading
  • Share-out: 2-3 volunteers share responses
  • Agenda review: Tell students what you'll do and why it matters
Main Activity (40-50 minutes)

Chunk instruction into 15-20 minute segments:

  • Segment 1: Mini-lecture or modeling (15 min)
  • Segment 2: Small group application or discussion (20 min)
  • Segment 3: Whole-class debrief and connection to upcoming work (15 min)

Vary activity types to maintain engagement. Mix lecture with discussion, individual work with group work.

Closing (10-15 minutes)
  • Exit ticket: Quick written response to check understanding
  • Preview next class and clarify homework expectations
  • Questions and clarifications
💡 Online/Hybrid Adaptation: For asynchronous components, record mini-lectures (10-12 min max), post discussion prompts with required responses, and use synchronous time for workshops, conferences, and collaborative work. Balance is key—don't just replicate face-to-face teaching online.

🤝 Building Classroom Community

Students write better when they feel safe taking risks, sharing drafts, and giving honest feedback. Establishing community early in the semester pays dividends in participation and learning.

Community-Building Strategies

Learn Names Quickly

  • Use name tents for first few weeks
  • Take photos (with permission) and quiz yourself
  • Play name games on Day 1
  • Greet students by name as they enter

Establish Norms Together

  • Co-create discussion guidelines (respect, listening, building on ideas)
  • Practice peer feedback protocols with low-stakes assignments first
  • Model the tone you want—respectful, curious, generous

Mix Up Groups Regularly

  • Use random grouping strategies
  • Rotate discussion partners each week
  • Prevent cliques from forming
  • Ensure quieter students aren't always grouped together

Share Your Own Writing

  • Show drafts, not just polished work
  • Talk about your own struggles as a writer
  • Invite feedback on your in-progress work
  • Model vulnerability and revision
"Students who feel they belong in a classroom are more likely to persist through challenges, seek help when needed, and engage deeply with course material. Community isn't a luxury—it's foundational to learning." — Educational research on belonging

💬 Effective Feedback Practices

Feedback is one of the most powerful tools for student learning, but it's also time-intensive. These strategies help you provide meaningful feedback efficiently.

Feedback Principles

1. Focus on Higher-Order Concerns (HOCs) First

Higher-Order: Thesis, argument, organization, evidence, analysis
Lower-Order: Grammar, punctuation, spelling, formatting

Students can't fix sentence-level errors if the whole paragraph needs to be rewritten. Address big-picture issues first, especially in early drafts.

2. Limit Your Comments

Research shows students are overwhelmed by extensive feedback. Instead:

  • Choose 2-3 key areas for improvement
  • Write marginal comments on select paragraphs, not every paragraph
  • End with a substantial end comment (3-5 sentences) identifying strengths and priorities for revision

3. Use Rubrics for Consistency

Rubrics make grading faster and more transparent. Students know expectations in advance. See the Assessment page for sample rubrics.

Time-Saving Feedback Strategies

📝 Comment Banks

Keep a document of frequently used comments you can copy/paste:

  • "Your thesis needs to be more specific. Instead of 'X is important,' try 'X matters because...'"
  • "You're summarizing here. Push into analysis: Why is this evidence significant?"
  • "Smooth transition needed—how does this paragraph connect to the previous one?"

🎥 Video or Audio Feedback

Record 3-5 minute video/audio comments instead of typing:

  • Often faster than writing
  • More conversational tone
  • Students can hear your emphasis and tone
  • Tools: Screencast-O-Matic, Loom, or Blackboard's audio comment feature

📋 Conferences

15-minute conferences can replace written feedback on drafts:

  • Students read a paragraph aloud
  • You discuss strengths and revision priorities
  • Student takes notes on action items
  • More efficient than extensive written comments

🔄 Revision Plans

Instead of correcting everything, ask students to create revision plans:

  • You identify 3 areas for revision
  • Students write a plan for how they'll address each
  • They submit the plan before the revised draft
💡 For ENG 0910 Students: Use workshop conferences to provide targeted feedback on works-in-progress. The embedded tutor can also help facilitate revision conversations, reducing your individual conference load.
