Assignment Overview

ENG 1010 requires students to complete at least 20 pages of finished formal writing over the course of the semester. The three assignments presented here offer a recommended sequence that moves students from close reading and critical analysis through synthesis and research-based argument. Instructors may adapt the sequence, assignment design, and topics while ensuring that students have opportunities to meet the official ENG 1010 learning outcomes.

The sample prompts, scaffolding strategies, and assignment-design principles on this page are intended as resources rather than required templates.

Essay 1: Close Reading & Critical Analysis (3-5 pages)

Purpose & Learning Outcomes

Essay 1 introduces students to academic writing by focusing on close reading, textual analysis, and evidence-based argumentation. Students learn to make claims about texts and support those claims with specific evidence.

LO 1: Rhetorical Situations LO 2: Authoritative Sources LO 3: Logical Arguments

Sample Prompts

Option A: Analyzing a Single Text

In Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein's "They Say / I Say," the authors argue that academic writing is fundamentally about entering conversations—responding to what "they say" while developing your own "I say." Choose one chapter or section from "They Say / I Say" and write an essay that analyzes how the authors use specific examples, templates, and explanations to develop their argument. What moves do Graff and Birkenstein make as writers to help readers understand their concepts? Where is their approach most effective, and where might readers still have questions? Use specific passages from the text to support your analysis.

Why this works: Focuses on the authors' rhetorical choices rather than just summarizing content. Students practice close reading while learning the concepts they'll use all semester.

Option B: Comparing Two Texts

Both [Author A] and [Author B] explore the concept of [theme/topic], but they approach it in different ways. Write an essay that analyzes how each author frames the issue, what evidence they use to support their claims, and what assumptions underlie their arguments. Which approach do you find more persuasive, and why? Use specific passages from both texts to support your analysis.

Why this works: Introduces comparative analysis while keeping the focus manageable. Students learn to identify patterns across texts.

Option C: Thematic Analysis

This semester, we're exploring the theme of [your course theme: education/identity/social justice/etc.]. Choose one reading from the first few weeks and write an essay that analyzes how the author addresses this theme. What specific argument does the author make? What rhetorical strategies (appeals to ethos, pathos, logos; use of examples, data, personal narrative) does the author employ? What assumptions does the author make about their audience? Use specific textual evidence to support your analysis.

Why this works: Connects to your course theme while teaching rhetorical analysis. Flexible enough to work with various readings.

Scaffolding Strategies

  • Annotation Workshop: Teach students to mark up texts actively (question marks, connections, key terms, confusing passages)
  • Claim Development Exercise: Have students write 3 possible thesis statements and workshop them in pairs
  • Evidence Selection: Students select 5-6 quotations and explain in writing why each is significant
  • Reverse Outline: After drafting, students create a reverse outline to check if each paragraph advances their argument
  • Peer Review Protocol: Focus on one element (thesis clarity, evidence quality, or analysis depth)
💡 Pro Tip: Many students struggle to move beyond summary in Essay 1. Encourage them to ask "So what?" after every piece of evidence. Why does this quotation matter? What does it reveal about the author's strategy or argument?
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Students often write plot summaries or biographical essays instead of analytical arguments. Require them to submit thesis statements early for feedback to ensure they're making arguable claims.

Essay 2: Synthesis & Argument (5-7 pages)

Purpose & Learning Outcomes

Essay 2 builds on the analytical work of Essay 1 by asking students to synthesize multiple sources, respond to different perspectives, and develop their own position within a conversation of ideas.

LO 1: Rhetorical Situations LO 2: Authoritative Sources LO 3: Logical Arguments LO 4: Language Conventions

Sample Prompts

Option A: Entering a Conversation

We've read several authors who discuss [your course theme/topic]. These writers don't always agree—some emphasize [perspective A] while others focus on [perspective B]. Write an essay that synthesizes the key points of at least three course readings and develops your own argument about [the issue]. Where do you see consensus among these writers? Where do they diverge? What perspective do you want to add to this conversation? Use specific evidence from at least three sources to support your argument.

Why this works: Students practice synthesis while developing their own position. The metaphor of "conversation" helps them see academic writing as dialogue rather than reporting.

Option B: Forwarding with a Difference

Choose one author from our course readings whose ideas you find compelling but incomplete. Write an essay that "forwards" this author's argument by extending, applying, or complicating it in a new context. For example, you might apply the author's framework to a case study they didn't consider, or you might argue that their insights need to be qualified based on evidence from other sources. Think about how Graff and Birkenstein describe entering conversations in "They Say / I Say"—you're building on someone else's ideas rather than just agreeing or disagreeing. Use at least three sources total (including the main author you're forwarding).

Why this works: Students learn that academic writing isn't just agreeing or disagreeing—it's building on others' ideas, a key concept from "They Say / I Say."

Option C: Problem-Posing Essay

Identify a problem or question that emerges from our course readings on [theme/topic]. Different authors might frame this problem differently or propose different solutions. Write an essay that defines the problem clearly, examines how at least three authors address it (or fail to address it), and develops your own response. What's at stake in how we understand and approach this problem?

Why this works: Encourages students to identify gaps and tensions in the readings rather than just summarizing agreement.

Scaffolding Strategies

  • Source Comparison Matrix: Students create a chart comparing how each source addresses key questions or themes
  • They Say / I Say Practice: Students write 3 paragraphs using templates from Graff and Birkenstein to practice integrating sources and entering conversations
  • Synthesis Statement Workshop: Students write one-sentence syntheses of groups of sources ("While X emphasizes A, Y and Z focus on B...")
  • Proposal Draft: Students submit a 1-page proposal outlining their argument and how they'll use each source
  • Citation Workshop: Focused session on MLA in-text citations and Works Cited formatting
💡 Pro Tip: Help students move beyond "X says this, Y says that, Z says this" structure by requiring them to identify patterns or tensions across sources. Use phrases like "While most scholars agree on X, they diverge on Y" to model synthesis.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Students often write "serial summary" essays (summarizing Source A, then B, then C) rather than true synthesis. Require a thesis that makes a claim ABOUT the sources, not just a claim supported BY sources.

Essay 3: Research-Based Argument (6-8 pages)

Purpose & Learning Outcomes

Essay 3 is the culminating project where students conduct independent research and make a sustained, complex argument. This assignment integrates all course learning outcomes and demonstrates students' ability to engage meaningfully with scholarly conversations.

LO 1: Rhetorical Situations LO 2: Authoritative Sources LO 3: Logical Arguments LO 4: Language Conventions

Sample Prompts

Option A: Open Inquiry Research

Develop a research question related to our course theme of [theme/topic] that genuinely interests you and requires investigation. Your question should be specific enough to answer in 6-8 pages but complex enough to require research beyond our course readings. Write an essay that synthesizes what scholars, experts, and other credible sources say about your question and develops your own argument in response. Use at least 6-8 sources, including at least 3 scholarly or peer-reviewed sources. Your essay should demonstrate your ability to enter an existing scholarly conversation, evaluate competing perspectives, and stake out your own position.

Why this works: Gives students ownership over their research while maintaining clear parameters. Emphasizes inquiry over reporting.

Option B: Contextualized Argument

We've explored [course theme] through various lenses this semester. Choose one specific case study, controversy, or contemporary issue related to this theme and write a research-based essay that helps readers understand what's at stake. Your essay should provide necessary background, synthesize what experts say about the issue, analyze the key tensions or disagreements, and make an argument about how we should understand or approach the issue. Use at least 6-8 credible sources, including scholarly articles, reputable journalism, and other authoritative voices.

Why this works: Balances expository and argumentative writing. Students practice synthesizing research while making their own claim.

Option C: Community-Based Research

Identify an issue affecting your campus, local community, or a community you're part of. Research what stakeholders, experts, and community members say about this issue. What solutions have been proposed? What tensions exist among different perspectives? Write an essay that explains the issue, synthesizes research and stakeholder perspectives, and makes an argument about how to address the problem. Use at least 6-8 sources, which may include scholarly research, journalism, policy documents, and interviews or surveys you conduct (if appropriate).

Why this works: Makes research feel relevant and urgent. Students can potentially use primary research (with appropriate support).

Multi-Stage Research Process

  • Week 1 - Topic Exploration: Students submit 3 possible research questions with preliminary source lists
  • Week 2 - Research Proposal: 1-2 page proposal outlining research question, why it matters, initial findings, and research plan
  • Week 3 - Annotated Bibliography: 6-8 sources with summaries and evaluations (75-100 words per source)
  • Week 4 - Source Synthesis: Students write 2 pages synthesizing what they've learned (no thesis yet—just mapping the conversation)
  • Week 5 - Argument Workshop: Students present 3-minute overviews of their emerging arguments; peers ask clarifying questions
  • Week 6 - Rough Draft: Complete draft for peer review (may be messy, but all sections present)
  • Week 7 - Final Draft Due: After revision and optional conferences
💡 Pro Tip: Build in multiple checkpoints so students can't procrastinate. Each checkpoint should be low-stakes (completion grades or completion + brief feedback) but required. This scaffolding is especially important for ENG 0910 students.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Students often choose topics that are too broad ("gun control," "climate change") or too narrow (hyper-specific local issues with no research available). Help them find the "Goldilocks zone" through the proposal process.

Source Requirements & Evaluation

Source Type Minimum Required What Counts
Scholarly/Peer-Reviewed 3 sources Academic journal articles, scholarly books, university press publications
Credible Popular Sources 3-5 sources Reputable journalism (NYT, The Atlantic, NPR), professional reports, .gov/.edu sites
Total Minimum 6-8 sources Mix of perspectives and source types

Teaching Source Evaluation: Don't just tell students to "use credible sources." Teach them how to evaluate:

  • Author credentials and institutional affiliation
  • Publication venue (peer-reviewed journal vs. blog)
  • Currency and relevance to their question
  • Use of evidence and citation practices
  • Potential bias or agenda (present in all sources—question is whether it's transparent)

Assignment Design Principles

Regardless of which prompts you choose, these principles will help you create effective assignments:

Make Goals Explicit

Tell students exactly what skills they're practicing and why. Connect each assignment to specific learning outcomes. Students write better when they understand the purpose beyond "getting a grade."

Scaffold Complex Tasks

Break major assignments into smaller steps with deadlines. This prevents procrastination, allows for iterative feedback, and helps students manage cognitive load. Especially critical for ENG 0910 students.

Build in Low-Stakes Practice

Before each major essay, include low-stakes writing (discussion posts, in-class writes, peer workshops). Students need opportunities to experiment without grade pressure.

Emphasize Revision

Real writers revise. Consider allowing one revision of any essay, having students write revision memos explaining their changes, or building revision into your process through required rough drafts.

Use Peer Review Strategically

Peer review works when it's structured. Give students specific tasks (find the thesis, identify where analysis is strong/weak) rather than "give feedback." Model what good feedback looks like.

Provide Mentor Texts

Share model essays (professional or strong student work from previous semesters, anonymized and with permission). Discuss what makes them effective. Students benefit from seeing examples of success.

💡 Customization for ENG 0910 Students: For students in the corequisite workshop, consider providing additional scaffolding like sentence starters for thesis statements, outline templates, or extra checkpoint conferences. Use workshop time for targeted support on assignment components.

Sequencing & Timing

Recommended Timeline for a 15-Week Semester:

  • Weeks 1-5: Essay 1 (Close Reading & Analysis)
  • Weeks 6-10: Essay 2 (Synthesis & Argument)
  • Weeks 11-15: Essay 3 (Research-Based Argument)

Build in flex time: Unexpected college closures, student absences, and the need for additional instruction on challenging concepts mean you should plan for some buffer. Don't schedule final essays due during the last week of classes if you can avoid it.

⚠️ Important Reminder: All assignments should explicitly connect to the four Learning Outcome categories outlined in the Course Overview: (1) Respond to Rhetorical Situations, (2) Engage with and Use Authoritative Sources, (3) Craft Logical Arguments, and (4) Apply Language Conventions. Consider including these on assignment sheets so students can track their skill development.
