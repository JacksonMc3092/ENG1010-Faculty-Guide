ENG 1010: Composition

Seven-week adjunct starter packet • Fall 2026, Session II (7B)

Suggested theme: What’s College For? Education, Opportunity, and Belonging

Course format: Two 150-minute meetings each week, Mondays and Wednesdays (seven instructional weeks).

Required text: Graff et al., They Say / I Say with Readings, 6th ed. (Norton, 2024); ISBN 9781324070139.

Dates: First class Monday, October 26; last class Monday, December 14, 2026. The official 7B session ends Tuesday, December 15.

Welcome

This is one approach to the course, not a prescribed one. It offers a reading path through the sixth edition of They Say / I Say with Readings, a complete sequence of class meetings, and assignments that can be adapted. Keep the activities you like; replace readings, themes, and prompts as needed while preserving opportunities for critical reading, argument, source use, and revision.

This particular model uses the anthology’s “What’s College For?” readings as one conversation: students analyze how an author makes an argument, synthesize different views of higher education, and then research a focused question that grows from the readings. The major essays are due in instructional Weeks 3, 5, and 7. Reading, drafting, feedback, and revision overlap across units.

A note about “Checkmarks”

Checkmarks is simply the name I use for low-stakes reading and writing activities. They can be assigned as homework or done during class. A Checkmark might ask students to annotate a passage, test a thesis, connect two readings, or gather evidence for an essay. I sometimes have students complete some Checkmarks in small groups when appropriate. They are not a required college-wide assignment type; feel free to rename, adjust, combine, or omit them. Their purpose is to give students practice and help them make progress toward larger projects.

How to use They Say / I Say

The book works well as a collection of writing moves rather than a chapter-by-chapter survey. Pair a short lesson or template exercise with an anthology reading and a task students can try in their own writing. Treat templates as tools students can adapt, not fixed formulas. Revisit the same chapters during drafting and revision.

Fourteen-meeting teaching calendar example

Each meeting includes suggested preparation, class work, and a writing move. Instructors can substitute activities or readings. The dates follow the Monday/Wednesday section; the final essay and reflection may be submitted in Blackboard on Tuesday, December 15, after the last class meeting.

Week 1 · Course launch and analysis

01 | Mon, Oct 26 — Entering a conversation

Prepare: Introduction (assigned after class); short instructor-selected excerpt or question about the purpose of college.

In class: Orient students to course and Blackboard. Begin with a low-pressure written response: what counts as learning, and where does it happen? Model how a writer starts with a shared belief and responds to it. Let students test one “they say / I say” pair in groups.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 1: introductory writing / response; collect during class to document early engagement.

02 | Wed, Oct 28 — Reading like a writer

Prepare: Ch. 1; Graff, “Hidden Intellectualism” (606–613); selected Ch. 14 guidance.

In class: Practice annotation, distinguish Graff’s central claim from his examples, and identify an assumption he challenges. Discuss the audience he addresses and how he tries to persuade that audience. Introduce Essay 1: analysis of an argument in “Hidden Intellectualism” or Rose’s essay.

Writing / next step: Mark one passage where Graff challenges a common belief about intellectual ability. After reading Rose, choose which essay to analyze.

03 | Mon, Nov 2 — From noticing to a working thesis

Prepare: Ch. 2; Rose, “Community College: The Great Equalizer?” (589–598).

In class: Compare how Graff and Rose frame intellectual ability and educational opportunity. Practice accurate summary, then ask how each writer uses examples, contrasts, or assumptions to make an argument. Students choose Graff or Rose for Essay 1 and test two analytical thesis statements.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 2: Essay 1 planning notes (chosen essay, author’s argument, intended audience, two passages worth analyzing, working thesis).

Week 2 · Drafting and evidence

04 | Wed, Nov 4 — Developing analytical paragraphs

Prepare: Ch. 3; reread the passages selected from Graff or Rose.

In class: Practice introducing and explaining quotations. Model the difference between stating what a writer argues and analyzing how the writer makes the case. Have students develop a body paragraph about a specific rhetorical choice or use of evidence. Run an Essay 1 draft workshop and focused peer review.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 3: two passages from Graff or Rose with notes explaining what each passage does in the author’s argument. Essay 1 rough draft for review.

Week 3 · Essay 1 final; synthesis begins

05 | Mon, Nov 9 — Revision and first synthesis moves

Prepare: Ch. 4; Clark, “Why We Need to Keep the ‘Community’ in Community Colleges” (599–605).

In class: Begin with an Essay 1 revision clinic (thesis, passages, explanation). Compare Clark’s emphasis on “community” with Rose’s account of opportunity and Graff’s view of intellectual life. Ask how a writer can extend or complicate an earlier claim. Introduce Essay 2: What should a college provide to make opportunity meaningful?

Writing / next step: Finish Essay 1 revisions; bring a question connecting two authors.

06 | Wed, Nov 11 — Whose perspectives are missing?

Prepare: Ch. 5; Lenga, “Disability in Higher Education” (566–577).

In class: Collect Essay 1 final. Discuss educational access and institutional assumptions without requiring personal disclosure. Connect Lenga to Clark, Rose, or Graff: what might each author say the others overlook? Develop an “I say” that responds to a real point of tension in two sources. Veterans Day: classes are held.

Writing / next step: Essay 1 final draft. Start Essay 2 source-conversation notes.

Week 4 · Synthesis and argument

07 | Mon, Nov 16 — An argument with a reasonable complication

Prepare: Ch. 6 and Ch. 8; Burwell, “Generation Stress” (578–588).

In class: Use Burwell alongside Rose, Clark, and Lenga to consider what colleges should provide beyond admission or classes. Group readings by shared issues (access, belonging, support, intellectual growth). Workshop an arguable Essay 2 thesis, a reasonable objection, and an explanation of why the question matters.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 4: source-conversation chart, working claim, and one complication.

08 | Wed, Nov 18 — Synthesis workshop

Prepare: Ch. 9 (selected); revisit Ch. 3 as needed.

In class: Model a synthesis paragraph combining evidence from two writers. Compare author-by-author and idea-based outlines. Hold peer review focused on thesis, source relationships, paragraph development, and what readers still need explained. End with time for a revision plan.

Writing / next step: Essay 2 rough draft and peer review; revise for Monday, Nov 23.

Week 5 · Essay 2 final; research begins

09 | Mon, Nov 23 — Finish the conversation; begin an inquiry

Prepare: Ch. 15 (opening sections); Lehman, “The Student Loan Trap” (614–624).

In class: Collect Essay 2 final. Introduce Essay 3: a research-supported argument about a focused question arising from the course conversation about education and opportunity. Use Lehman’s discussion of student debt to demonstrate the move from a published argument to a researchable question. Begin a library or database search demonstration.

Writing / next step: Essay 2 final draft due Monday, Nov 23. Identify a possible research issue or question and the course reading that sparked it. No class Wednesday, Nov 25 (Thanksgiving recess).

Week 6 · Inquiry and evidence

10 | Mon, Nov 30 — What counts as useful research?

Prepare: Opoku-Agyeman and Addo, “Student Loan Forgiveness Critics Are Wrong…” (625–632); Ch. 15.

In class: Compare how Lehman and Opoku-Agyeman/Addo frame student debt and support their positions. Practice evaluating claims, counterarguments, and evidence. Use library sources to investigate a narrower related question. Students may choose other education-related issues, or another approved topic if it supports the course outcomes.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 5: focused question, a relevant course reading, and an initial log of two possible outside sources with notes on their usefulness.

11 | Wed, Dec 2 — Gathering evidence and building the draft

Prepare: Ch. 3 and Ch. 15 (review); student-selected sources.

In class: Workshop course readings, outside research, and relevant evidence together. Practice signal phrases, quotation, paraphrase, and MLA citation. Test a tentative answer to the research question and develop at least two body paragraphs. Use brief conferences to discuss argument, scope, or source gaps.

Writing / next step: Checkmark 6: evidence log with four to six quotations, paraphrases, facts, or examples from course and outside sources, noting how each supports or complicates the argument. Essay 3 partial draft.

Week 7 · Essay 3 draft, revision, reflection

12 | Mon, Dec 7 — From partial draft to full argument

Prepare: Ch. 12 (revision); no heavy new anthology reading.

In class: Run a full-draft workshop: reverse outline, mark unsupported claims, and ask whether the course readings and outside research genuinely speak to one another. Peer exchange should surface questions a thoughtful reader might raise. Confer with writers whose claim or scope is still unsettled.

Writing / next step: Essay 3 complete rough draft.

13 | Wed, Dec 9 — Revision with readers in mind

Prepare: Ch. 12 (review); draft and rubric.

In class: Hold the major peer-review workshop. Prioritize thesis, idea-based organization, integration of assigned and outside sources, and explanation of evidence before sentence editing. Students write a short plan naming two or three changes and begin making them.

Writing / next step: Peer review and revision plan (not a new major assignment).

14 | Mon, Dec 14 — Completing and reflecting

Prepare: No substantial new reading; review MLA and course outcomes as needed.

In class: Reserve most class time for conferences, finishing revisions, and a short reflection. Ask students to name a writing move they now use deliberately, show an example from their work, and describe how their thinking changed. Close the conversation without introducing another unit.

Writing / next step: Essay 3 final draft and course reflection due Tuesday, Dec 15 via Blackboard (submission time set by instructor); final in-person class is Monday, Dec 14.

Adaptable major writing assignments

Essay 1 · How a writer challenges assumptions about education

Working prompt: Choose Gerald Graff’s “Hidden Intellectualism” or Mike Rose’s “Community College: The Great Equalizer?” Both writers invite readers to reconsider ideas about learning and educational opportunity. Write an essay that analyzes how your chosen author builds that argument for a particular audience. What widely held view does the writer respond to? How do examples, contrasts, reasoning, language, or other choices help make the case? Develop a specific claim about how the argument works, supporting it with close analysis of passages rather than a sequence of summaries or a personal agree/disagree response.

Support in the calendar: Read and annotate Graff/Rose → identify an assumption and audience → choose two or three significant passages → draft an analytical claim → workshop the draft → revise and submit (Nov 11).

Essay 2 · What should college provide?

Working prompt: Rose and Clark raise questions about what it means for a community college to provide genuine opportunity; Graff, Lenga, and Burwell add perspectives on intellectual life, access, and support. Using at least three assigned readings, develop your own answer to a focused question such as: Is access to college enough, or what else helps students thrive? Which responsibilities should colleges prioritize? Put authors into conversation around shared issues and meaningful disagreements. Explain what your argument adds rather than reporting each reading separately.

Support in the calendar: Connect at least three authors → frame one debatable question → develop a thesis → consider an objection or complication → synthesize evidence in paragraphs → peer review → revise and submit (Nov 23).

Essay 3 · Researching a question about educational opportunity

Working prompt: Lehman and Opoku-Agyeman/Addo offer contrasting arguments about student debt and who benefits from public policy. Use their disagreement—or another issue raised by our course readings—as the starting point for a focused research question about higher education. You might investigate student-loan policy, textbook costs, transfer pathways, academic advising, disability access, childcare, or support for working students. Develop a thesis-driven argument that draws on at least one relevant course reading and credible outside research. Present the problem fairly, consider a thoughtful competing perspective, and explain what your evidence leads you to conclude. The instructor may approve another focused topic.

Support in the calendar: Question growing from a reading → preliminary source search → evidence and source log → working claim → partial draft → complete draft → peer review → substantial revision → final submission (Dec 15).

Instructor note: These are usable examples, not required departmental prompts. Before distributing them, decide the scope, length, source minimum, grading criteria, and submission rules. For Essay 3, a practical starting point is at least one course reading and two credible outside sources, with a brief explanation of how sources will be assessed. Give students room to develop a question they care about, while keeping the assignment’s focus on inquiry, evidence, and argument.

Keep the grading light. Depending on the instructor’s approach, a Checkmark can be completion-based, discussed and returned, or simply used during class. It is not necessary to write extensive individual feedback on every short activity.

A workable 150-minute class rhythm

15 minutes: opening writing and review; 35 minutes: close reading and discussion; 25 minutes: short writing lesson and practice; 10-minute break; 50 minutes: collaborative work, drafting, peer review, or conferences; 15 minutes: reflection and next steps. This is an example, not a fixed schedule. Workshop days can devote most of the meeting to writing and conferencing.

What can change—and what to keep

The instructor can change the theme, choose different anthology readings, substitute media, rename Checkmarks, reorder individual writing lessons, or revise the sample prompts. The key through-line is repeated practice with meaningful texts: understand a claim, analyze how it is made, connect writers’ perspectives, investigate a question, and revise work with readers in mind.
