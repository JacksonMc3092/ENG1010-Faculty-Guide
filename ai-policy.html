AI Guidance for ENG 1010

Generative artificial intelligence is changing how students read, write, research, and work with information. ENG 1010 faculty should establish clear expectations for whether and how students may use AI tools in their courses and assignments.

CT State and CSCU guidance surrounding artificial intelligence continues to evolve. Faculty should consult current institutional resources and academic integrity guidance when developing course policies. This page provides ENG 1010 teaching guidance and sample approaches rather than an institution-wide AI policy.

AI in the Writing Classroom

Generative AI tools can support brainstorming, drafting, revision, summarizing, research, and other writing tasks. They also raise important questions about authorship, source use, verification, academic integrity, and the purposes of writing assignments.

  • What kinds of AI use support the learning goals of an assignment?
  • What kinds of AI use replace the thinking or writing an assignment is designed to assess?
  • How should students acknowledge or cite AI assistance?
  • How can students verify AI-generated claims, sources, quotations, and citations?

Faculty should establish clear expectations for AI use in the syllabus and, when appropriate, in individual assignments. Those expectations should remain aligned with current CT State and CSCU academic integrity guidance.

Sample Approaches to Course AI Policies

Faculty may take different approaches to generative AI depending on their course design, assignments, and learning goals. The examples below are starting points that instructors may adapt.

Approach 1: Prohibition

Policy: "The use of generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) to generate any portion of assignments in this course constitutes academic dishonesty."

Rationale: Writing is thinking. Using AI to generate text short-circuits the learning process. Students need to struggle with ideas and language to develop as writers.

Considerations: Difficult to enforce. Students who use AI anyway may have an unfair advantage. Doesn't prepare students for workplaces where AI use is expected.

Approach 2: Selective Permission

Policy: "You may use AI tools for brainstorming, outlining, and editing grammar, but all final drafts must be your own words and ideas. You must cite any AI assistance in an 'AI Use Statement' at the end of your essay."

Rationale: Mirrors how professionals use AI—as a tool, not a replacement for human thinking. Teaches responsible AI use while maintaining focus on original thinking.

Considerations: Requires clear guidelines on what's permissible. Students may struggle to distinguish between "brainstorming" and "drafting."

Approach 3: Transparent Integration

Policy: "AI tools are permitted throughout your writing process. You must document and reflect on how you used AI tools in each assignment, explaining what you generated with AI, what you wrote yourself, and how you evaluated and revised AI-generated content."

Rationale: AI is here to stay. Our job is to teach students to be critical users of AI, not to pretend it doesn't exist. Focus shifts from "did you use AI?" to "how well did you use AI?"

Considerations: Requires rethinking what we assess. Focuses on synthesis, evaluation, and revision skills rather than text generation alone.

Clarity and Consistency: These examples represent different approaches faculty may consider. Whatever approach an instructor adopts, expectations should be clearly communicated to students, applied consistently, and aligned with current CT State and CSCU academic integrity guidance.

Crafting Your AI Policy

If you address generative AI in your syllabus or assignments, make the expectations concrete enough that students can tell what is permitted, what must be acknowledged, and what they are responsible for verifying.

Three useful anchors: Permission — explain what kinds of AI use are allowed or not allowed. Acknowledgment — explain when and how students should disclose or cite AI assistance. Verification — remind students that they remain responsible for checking claims, quotations, sources, citations, and other material produced with AI assistance.

1. Define What You Mean by "AI Tools"

Be specific. Do you mean:

  • Text generation tools? (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Jasper)
  • Grammar checkers? (Grammarly, ProWritingAid)
  • Paraphrasing tools? (QuillBot, WordTune)
  • Translation tools? (Google Translate, DeepL)

Students need clarity on what's in bounds vs. out of bounds.

2. Explain Your Rationale

Help students understand why you're setting this policy. Connect it to learning outcomes:

  • "This course focuses on developing your ability to construct arguments. AI can generate text, but it can't develop your thinking."
  • "Learning to write well requires struggle and revision. AI can short-circuit that process."
  • "Part of college is learning to use tools responsibly. We'll explore how AI can support—not replace—your writing."

3. Specify Consequences

What happens if students violate your policy? Be clear:

  • First offense: Redo the assignment? Zero on the assignment? Meeting to discuss?
  • Repeated offenses: Failing grade? Academic integrity violation reported to Dean?

4. Provide Examples (If Permitting Some Use)

Show students what responsible AI use looks like:

  • Acceptable: "I used ChatGPT to generate 5 possible thesis statements, then chose one and significantly revised it to better fit my argument."
  • Not Acceptable: "I pasted the assignment prompt into ChatGPT and submitted the essay it generated."

Practical Strategies

Regardless of your policy, here are strategies to navigate the AI era:

Design Assignments that Emphasize Student Thinking

  • Make it personal: Ask students to connect readings to their own experiences
  • Use unfamiliar texts: AI is trained on published content; unique class readings are harder for it to engage with
  • Require process documentation: Drafts, annotated bibliographies, peer review—AI can't fake the process
  • Emphasize synthesis over summary: AI can summarize well but struggles with original synthesis
  • Focus on local/specific topics: AI knowledge is general; specific case studies are harder

Emphasize In-Class Writing

Increase the weight of in-class writing, timed essays, and exam components. These demonstrate a student's unaided abilities and provide a baseline for comparison with take-home work.

Have Conversations, Not Just Policies

Dedicate class time to discussing AI tools:

  • What can AI do well? What can't it do?
  • When is it helpful vs. harmful to use AI in writing?
  • What are the ethical implications of AI-generated content?
  • How do we distinguish between AI assistance and AI plagiarism?

Frame it as a learning opportunity, not just a policing issue.

Teach AI Literacy

Consider making AI literacy part of your curriculum:

  • Have students prompt AI to write an essay, then critique it
  • Teach students to fact-check AI output (AI hallucinates sources!)
  • Discuss when AI-generated text is detectable and why
  • Explore the limitations of AI in understanding nuance, context, and original thought
⚠️ AI Detection Requires Caution: AI-detection scores should not be treated as proof that a student used generative AI. If a submission raises concerns, consider the student’s writing process, prior work, sources, drafts, and other available evidence, and follow the same faculty-first conversation process described on the Policies page.
